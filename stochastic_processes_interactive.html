How to read the current result

Read these four observations from top to bottom, then move an anchor or change the process model and compare again.

1
What the two histograms show

Blue collects X(first anchor time) across the ensemble; coral collects X(second anchor time). Each path contributes one value to each histogram. More overlap means the two fixed-time empirical distributions are more alike.

2
What to compare first

Compare whether the histogram shapes overlap, then compare their means and variances in the table. Close—but not necessarily identical—values support stability because the ensemble is finite.

3
“Same-lag covariance” in plain language

Compare X(first anchor time) with X(first anchor time + common lag), then repeat from the second anchor time. Covariance describes how strongly observations the same distance apart tend to vary together across realizations.

4
What conclusion is reasonable

Substantial histogram overlap and similar means, variances, and same-lag covariances support wide-sense stationarity. Systematic shifts or spreading challenge it; small mismatches may come from a finite ensemble.

This is an empirical diagnostic: it illustrates marginal stability and wide-sense stationarity conditions. It does not, by itself, prove strict-sense stationarity.

Three meanings of stationarity

Marginal stationarity: the distribution of X(t) does not depend on the time index.

Wide-sense stationarity: the mean is constant and autocovariance depends only on the lag, not on the absolute time.

Strict-sense stationarity: every finite-dimensional joint distribution is invariant under a common time shift. Equal one-time PDFs are therefore necessary marginal evidence, but they are not sufficient by themselves to establish strict-sense stationarity.

Small empirical differences between slices are expected because a finite ensemble has Monte Carlo variability.
